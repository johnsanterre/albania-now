3 · Read & play

Chunks, not letters; a desk, not a memory

Before the model sees your message, a pre-processor chops it into tokens — chunks of text. Common short words are usually one token each. Longer or rarer words get split: strawberry might be straw+berry. The model receives numbered IDs for those chunks. It never sees the letters inside them.

Feed it something and watch the chopping happen:

Type anything. The top row is how the text gets chopped; the bottom row is what the model actually receives.

WHAT THE MODEL SEES:

Try: Mississippi · bookkeeper · your own name · brontofraggle. Notice the bottom row every time — where did the letters go?

That's why letter-level tasks trip it: counting letters, reversing words, precise acrostics. You're asking about detail that exists below its vision. There's a workaround — make it spell the word letter by letter first (each letter becomes its own token, now visible) — and the workaround proves the diagnosis.

Tokens also set the model's memory. Every model has a context window: the maximum tokens it can consider at once — its desk size. Your conversation, your pasted documents, its own replies — all of it must fit on the desk. When a chat gets long, the earliest material slides off the edge and is simply gone. The model isn't ignoring you; it literally no longer has the beginning.

Two practical habits fall out of this. Restate what matters: in a long session, re-paste the key facts instead of trusting the desk. Diagnose weird failures at the right level: if the task lives in letters, expect trouble and force the spelling trick.

4 · Try it

Find its blind spots

1. The count. Ask it to count letters in five words — try strawberry, Mississippi, bookkeeper, and two of your own. Score it.

2. The fix. For any miss, say: "Spell the word one letter per line, then count." Watch the accuracy change, and explain to yourself why.

3. The reversal. Ask it to spell a long word backwards, fast. Then ask the same with the one-letter-per-line trick.

If it gets everything right: newer models have been specifically trained around these famous failures — that's not vision, it's memorized workarounds. Push harder: invent a word it has never seen (brontofraggle), ask it to count letters across a whole sentence at once, or ask which is alphabetically first among five made-up words. The blindness is still there; the workarounds only cover the famous cases.
