3 · Read & play

No database inside

A language model is a next-word predictor. It was trained by reading an enormous amount of text — books, websites, code, conversations — and practicing one skill over and over: given everything so far, guess what comes next. Billions of practice rounds later, that guessing got extremely good.

When you send it a message, it does not search a database of saved answers. There is no database. It writes its reply into existence one piece at a time, each piece chosen because it is a plausible continuation of everything before it. The answer to your question did not exist until the moment it was generated.

Before reading another word about the trick — do the trick. You already have the skill; you've been training on English your whole life.

Be the model

Five sentences stop mid-thought. Type the single word you'd bet comes next, then reveal what a language model would bet.

Round 1 of 5

Those percentage bars are the whole machine. At every step the model holds a ranked list like that over every word it knows, reshaped by everything that came before. Notice how the shape changed round to round: sometimes one word takes nearly all the probability, sometimes the bet is spread wide — and being unsure was the correct bet for an open sentence.

"phone" is in the running for everyone — everything is always a little bit possible. What the context changes is the ranking.

This explains three things you'll see constantly:

Flexibility. Poems, business plans, apologies, code — it's all just text to continue. One trick covers all of it.

Confidence without guarantee. A false sentence can be perfectly plausible. The machine that writes truths and the machine that writes mistakes are the same machine, running the same trick.

Different answers to the same question. There's deliberate randomness in how it picks among good next words — it keeps the writing from going stale. Ask twice, get two answers. Neither is "the" answer; both are draws from the same distribution. Figure 3 is that machine, small enough to see through:

    Everything the real thing does, this toy does: ranked bets, a draw, and a sentence that didn't exist until it was written. The real model's vocabulary is ~100,000 pieces instead of a few dozen, and its ranking machinery took months of training — but you have now seen the entire loop.

    Hold on to this mental model. Every lesson in this course — tokens, training, prompting, hallucination — is a consequence of it.

    4 · Try it

    Catch the real one predicting

    The figures above were toys with a few dozen words. Now run the same three experiments on the real thing — open the AI chatbot your class uses:

    1. The continuation. Send exactly this: "Continue this sentence in ten different ways: The best thing about Chicago is" — you're watching raw prediction with the mask off. Compare its ten to Figure 3's little set.

    2. The double ask. Ask the same interesting question twice in two separate chats. Compare the answers. Same machine, different draws — Figure 3's dial, hidden from view.

    3. The impossible lookup. Ask: "What did I have for breakfast today?" A good model admits it can't know. Notice what that tells you: it has no access to anything but the text you give it.

    Go deeper — optional, for when the lesson wasn't enough

    More of the machine, if you want it

    Watch the idea again, bigger. Search YouTube for 3Blue1Brown's "Large language models explained briefly" — the same next-word machine, beautifully animated, in seven minutes.
    A long read that goes all the way down. Stephen Wolfram's essay "What Is ChatGPT Doing … and Why Does It Work?" (free online) walks from next-word betting to the full architecture. Ambitious, readable.
    Stretch. Your phone keyboard's suggestion bar is a tiny language model. Start a sentence, accept the middle suggestion ten times, and compare the result with Figure 3's toy. Same trick, different scale.
